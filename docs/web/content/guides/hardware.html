Choose your hardware

ESPectre supports six ESP32 chip families. A compact board is usually enough for a dedicated sensor.

Supported chips

All chips below use the same detection pipeline. ESP32-S2 runs ESPHome and Native, but not Matter or Micro-ESPectre; the other listed chips also have Matter and Micro-ESPectre support.

Short answer: for a new build, start with a compact ESP32-S3 board. It offers the most compute headroom without requiring a full-size development kit. Choose ESP32-C6 when the wider product needs 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi 6, Thread, or Zigbee, and ESP32-C5 when dual-band or 5 GHz work is the reason for the hardware choice.
Supported ESP32 chip families and practical selection notes
ChipPractical notes
ESP32-S3The general recommendation for a new sensor. Its dual-core CPU runs at up to 240 MHz, and its 128-bit vector instructions include multiplication operations that can accelerate optimized signal-processing and ML code. ESPectre does not require that acceleration, but the extra headroom is useful when the board also runs product logic or future processing workloads.
ESP32-C6Choose it when the wider product benefits from 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi 6, Thread, or Zigbee. ESPectre currently pins sensing traffic to the validated HT20 802.11n profile, so the detector does not yet use 802.11ax.
ESP32-C5The choice for dual-band Wi-Fi 6 or 5 GHz development. Native firmware defaults to 2.4 GHz; ESPHome defaults to automatic band selection when wifi.band_mode is omitted, although the maintained examples select 2.4 GHz. Device settings does not change the band. ESPectre can use 5 GHz HT20, but detection quality on 5 GHz is not yet characterized.
ESP32-C3Small, inexpensive, and widely available. A Mini or SuperMini-style board is sufficient for a dedicated ESPectre sensor when its antenna and power supply suit the installation.
ESP32-S2A single-core Wi-Fi option with no Bluetooth radio. Use Improv Serial or the ESPHome fallback access point for provisioning; Matter is not available.
ESP32Widely available and well supported. Fine for reusing boards you already own.

What matters

  • The antenna: an external antenna connector (U.FL/IPEX) lets you choose and position the antenna independently of the board. This can help when the enclosure, mounting position, or link distance makes the built-in PCB antenna difficult to place.
  • The power supply: use a stable supply rated for the board; USB-powered boards normally expect 5 V. If readings remain erratic in an otherwise good position, compare them with another power adapter.
  • The placement: keep the sensor out of metal cabinets and, when the room allows it, start 3–8 m from the access point. The placement guide covers distance, orientation, obstacles, and room testing in detail.
  • The programming connection: if the board exposes USB, use a data cable for flashing. Smaller boards may instead require a compatible serial or JTAG adapter.
Treat one board per room as a starting point, then test the actual coverage boundary. Every published firmware can operate alongside other ESPectre devices.
