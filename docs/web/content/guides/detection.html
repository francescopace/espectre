How motion detection works

What the movement score actually measures, what happens during calibration, and how to place and tune the sensor so it behaves well in your room.

The idea in one minute

Wi-Fi signals reach the ESP32 over many paths at once: direct, bounced off walls, furniture, and people. Each received packet carries Channel State Information (CSI), a per-subcarrier measurement of how the radio channel looked at that instant. When nobody moves, those measurements are remarkably stable. When a person moves, the reflected paths change and the measurements start to scatter. ESPectre measures that scatter, on the device, packet by packet.

The signal path

Everything below runs on the ESP32. No raw radio data leaves the device; only the derived score and motion state are published.

Wi-Fi packetsHT20 traffic
CSI captureSubcarrier samples
ConditioningNormalize and filter
DetectionClassic or ML
OutputScore and motion

Conditioning matters because raw CSI is noisy: gain normalization compensates for the receiver's automatic gain control, a Hampel filter drops isolated outliers, and an optional low-pass filter removes fast noise while keeping the slower variations caused by human movement.

What the numbers mean

  • Movement score (0.0–1.0): how strongly the channel is changing right now. It is a room-specific signal, not a physical unit: 0.3 in your living room and 0.3 in your office are not the same thing.
  • Threshold: the score level that counts as motion. Classic adapts it during calibration; you can adjust it at runtime from Home Assistant, the Configure page, or MQTT.
  • Motion state: turns on after the score stays above the threshold for a few consecutive evaluations (about one second at default settings) and off after it stays below (slightly faster). This hit filter is why a single spike does not trigger a false alarm.
  • Calibration: Classic learns a quiet threshold at startup, taking up to about ten seconds at the default packet rate. ML uses its trained threshold and does not run this calibration, although its feature window still needs to fill.

Both are production detectors, run entirely on the ESP32, and use the same 0.0–1.0 scale. ESPHome and Native can switch between them at runtime and persist the selection; Matter supports both as build-time choices but does not switch at runtime.

Placement that works

  • Keep the sensor roughly 3–8 meters from your Wi-Fi access point. Too close risks receiver saturation; too far means a weaker, noisier signal.
  • Put it in the room you want to monitor, where people cross the space between the router and the sensor or move near the device.
  • Do not enclose it in a metal cabinet or press the antenna against a large metal surface.
  • When using Classic, keep the room still during startup calibration.
  • Moved the device? Recalibrate Classic. With ML, let the feature window refill and repeat the same quiet-and-motion test.

For antenna orientation, RSSI ranges, room layouts, and a repeatable comparison procedure, follow the dedicated sensor placement guide.

Quick tuning recipe

Triggers with nobody there

  • Raise the threshold a little.
  • With Classic, recalibrate with the room genuinely empty.
  • Look for fans, curtains in airflow, or pets: the sensor sees them too.

Misses real motion

  • Lower the threshold a little.
  • Check placement: distance from the AP and obstacles matter most.
  • Verify the device receives steady traffic (the built-in traffic generator keeps ~100 packets/s by default).
Wi-Fi sensing detects changes in the radio environment. It does not identify people, count them, or replace a safety-certified presence sensor.
